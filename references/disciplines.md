# Discipline conventions

Every discipline has its own vocabulary, voice and evidence habits. Keep the discipline's terms and conventions; edit the language around them.

## Science, engineering and computing

- Passive voice is common in methods ("The samples were heated to 80 °C"); active voice with *we* is accepted in many fields. Follow the text and the brief.
- Keep equations, variables, units, symbols and figure and table numbers exactly as written.
- Distinguish measured results, calculated values, simulations, estimates and design targets.
- Code, identifiers and file names stay in their original form.

## Business and management

- Name frameworks correctly (for example SWOT, PESTLE, Porter's Five Forces) and apply them to the case rather than describing them generally.
- Recommendations should be specific, prioritised and justified by the analysis.
- Separate data from the case, external evidence and the writer's assumptions.

## Law

- Keep case names, statute titles, section numbers and court citations exactly as given; case names are usually italicised in the reference style used.
- Distinguish the rule, its application to the facts and the conclusion. Say who is being advised.
- Avoid stating a legal outcome as certain when it depends on missing facts or on judicial discretion.
- Jurisdiction matters: do not merge rules from different jurisdictions.

## Humanities

- Argument and interpretation are central; the writer's voice and position should remain visible.
- Quotations must stay exact; use the required style's rules for quotation marks and page numbers.
- First person may be acceptable; follow the brief.

## Social sciences and education

- Distinguish findings, interpretations and theoretical claims.
- Use the discipline's terms for methods (for example *qualitative*, *thematic analysis*, *sample*, *participants*).
- Use respectful, current terms for people and groups; follow the brief or the style guide named in it.

## Health and life sciences

- Keep drug names, doses, units and statistical results exact.
- Use person-first or identity-first language according to the field's guidance and the brief.
- Avoid overstating causation from correlational or small-sample evidence.
