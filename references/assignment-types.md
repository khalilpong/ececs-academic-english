# Assignment types

Typical structure, tone and pitfalls for common written assignments. Always follow the actual brief or template first. Do not restructure a document unless asked; suggest changes instead.

## Lab and technical reports

- **Structure:** title, abstract or summary, introduction and aim, method, results, discussion, conclusion, references, appendices.
- **Tone:** factual and precise. Method usually in past tense; established facts in present tense.
- **Pitfalls:** interpretation mixed into results; results that do not answer the aim; units, uncertainties or figure numbers missing; conclusions stronger than the data.

## Design and project reports

- **Structure:** problem and requirements, options considered, selection rationale, design detail, testing or validation, evaluation, recommendations.
- **Tone:** decision-focused: each choice is justified against requirements or criteria.
- **Pitfalls:** describing features without justifying them; planned tests written as if completed; targets presented as achieved results.

## Argumentative and discursive essays

- **Structure:** introduction with a clear position, body paragraphs each making one point with evidence, counter-argument and response, conclusion.
- **Tone:** reasoned and evaluative; the writer's position is visible.
- **Pitfalls:** description instead of argument; topic sentences that do not state the point; a conclusion that adds new claims.

## Literature reviews

- **Structure:** scope and method of search, themes or debates (not one paragraph per source), gaps, implications for the study.
- **Tone:** synthesis: compare and evaluate sources rather than summarise them one by one.
- **Pitfalls:** "X said… Y said…" lists; uncritical acceptance; reporting verbs that misstate a source's certainty.

## Case-study and problem-question answers

Common in law, ethics, business and management.

- **Structure:** for each issue, identify the issue, state the relevant concept, rule or framework, apply it to the given facts, and conclude or advise. In law this is often called IRAC.
- **Tone:** advisory: make clear who is being advised and what the advice is.
- **Pitfalls:** discussing facts that are not given; ignoring facts that are; using a framework without explaining it first; not stating where missing facts would change the conclusion; treating one party's claim as established fact.

## Reflective writing

- **Structure:** often follows a model named in the brief (for example, describe, analyse, evaluate, plan).
- **Tone:** first person is normally expected; honest and specific, with analysis rather than narrative.
- **Pitfalls:** too much description; generic lessons; changing the writer's actual experiences when polishing.

## Research proposals

- **Structure:** background and rationale, research questions or aims, method, ethics, timeline, expected outcomes, references.
- **Tone:** future tense for planned work; feasible and specific.
- **Pitfalls:** promising results; vague methods; aims that do not match the questions.

## Abstracts and executive summaries

- **Structure:** purpose, method, key results with numbers, conclusion or recommendation, in one or two paragraphs.
- **Tone:** dense but readable; no citations unless the brief requires them.
- **Pitfalls:** background only, no results; claims not supported in the main text; exceeding the word limit.

## Dissertation or thesis chapters

- Apply the matching section type above (literature review, method, results, discussion).
- Keep terminology, abbreviations and tense consistent with earlier chapters.

## Posters and presentation text

- Short, parallel phrases; one idea per bullet; numbers with units.
- Keep claims as calibrated as in the full report.
