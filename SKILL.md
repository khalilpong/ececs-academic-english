# ECECS Academic English

Apply the user's ECECS Pro language brief: undergraduate academic writing, approximately IELTS 6.5–7.5 language complexity, and natural rather than mechanical expression. This is a style target, not a predicted examination score or an official institutional standard.

## Work within the requested scope

- Read the supplied text and any current assignment-specific language requirements. Those requirements and the user's requested scope take precedence over these defaults.
- For polishing, make the smallest useful changes. Preserve the author's argument, organisation, technical terminology, numbers, units, equations, citations and intended meaning. Do not rewrite a whole section when local editing will suffice.
- If no text is supplied, request the passage or document. If the task is explanation or feedback, give that rather than silently producing a replacement draft.

## Language decisions

- Choose familiar, precise academic words. Keep necessary disciplinary terms; do not replace them with less accurate synonyms merely to simplify the prose.
- Vary sentence length where the reasoning benefits. Split overloaded sentences, identify vague subjects, and remove redundant framing. Do not force a word-count limit per sentence or ban complex sentences.
- Use active or passive voice according to emphasis and clarity. Avoid unnecessary nominalisation without treating passive voice or noun phrases as inherently wrong.
- Give each paragraph a clear central point. Connect evidence, interpretation and implications where relevant, without imposing the same paragraph template everywhere.
- Use transitions only when they express an actual relationship. Remove repetitive signposting, generic praise, inflated novelty claims and empty conclusions. Do not rely on a fixed blacklist of supposedly AI-associated words.
- Preserve the document's English variety. If none is established, British spelling is the project-derived default; a different user or assignment preference overrides it.

## Preserve the strength of each claim

- Keep verified facts distinct from interpretations, proposals, targets, assumptions and evidence gaps. Do not turn a planned test into a completed experiment or a scenario into a forecast.
- Use qualification only when justified. “May” and “could” can express uncertainty, but do not weaken a mandatory “must” requirement or add uncertainty to an established fact without reason.
- Keep qualifications, conditions, denominators and limitations attached to the relevant claims. If changing them would affect meaning, flag the issue instead of silently editing it.
- Language review is not source verification. Do not invent citations, findings or missing support. Preserve required citation style and flag unsupported claims separately.

## Deliver the requested result

For a short passage, provide the revision and briefly explain only meaningful changes. For a document review, identify locations and actionable edits. If asked for a file edit, preserve its layout and use the available document workflow; this skill does not prescribe a file library.

Before returning a revision, compare it with the original for changes to meaning, numerical values, technical scope, certainty, tense and citations. Report unresolved content questions separately from stylistic changes. Stop when the prose is clear; do not create successive versions merely to vary wording.

Natural expression means readable, specific prose. Do not add deliberate errors, promise detector avoidance, conceal actual AI assistance, or present this skill as permission to submit AI-generated coursework. Follow the applicable assignment's authorship and disclosure rules; do not import another course's platform or submission rules.
